---
title: Taking a trade without lying to the customer or the book
summary: The trade is where most deals die and most reviews get written. How to give a real number and have it survive the conversation.
---

The trade appraisal is the most emotional part of the deal. The customer has a number in their head from a website, and that number is usually high. How you handle the gap decides whether you keep the deal.

## Never insult the car

The oldest move in the business is running the car down to justify a low number. Every scuff gets pointed at, every sound gets a frown.

Customers have heard about this move. The moment you start, they stop believing you, and they start believing the website that told them their car was worth four thousand more than you just offered.

Say what is good about the car first. It is usually true — they took care of it, or it is a desirable model, or the miles are low. Then explain the number.

## Explain where the number comes from

A trade number is not a mystery. Walk them through it:

- What similar cars are actually selling for around here, not asking prices
- What this specific one needs before it can go on the line — tires, brakes, detail, any mechanical work
- What it costs to hold it while it sells

"Your car retails around eleven. It needs tires and a brake job, that's about nine hundred. Then we have to make something on it. That's how I get to my number." That is a conversation a reasonable person can follow, even when they do not love the answer.

## About the website estimate

They will bring one. Do not attack it — explain what it is.

Those numbers are generated from a description the customer typed in, usually with condition set to "excellent," sight unseen, and often for a private sale rather than a trade. It is a starting point, not an offer. The honest framing:

> "That's a fair ballpark for a private sale if you want to list it, take the calls, and deal with the test drives. What I can do is take it today, and that convenience is part of the difference."

That is true, it respects their research, and it gives them a real choice.

## When they still owe money

Do not dance around it. Get the payoff amount from their lender before you talk numbers, because everything changes if they are upside down.

If they owe more than the car is worth, say the words plainly: "You owe about two thousand more than it's worth. That doesn't kill the deal, but that amount has to go somewhere — either you bring it, or it rolls into the new loan and you pay interest on it." Let them decide with real information.

Rolling negative equity silently into a payment is how you get a very angry phone call in month three.

## Do not promise what the book has not said

Never give a number before you have looked at the car. Never give a range to get them in the door and then come down when they arrive. That is the single fastest way to earn a one-star review that mentions bait and switch by name.

If you need time, say you need time: "Give me fifteen minutes with it and I'll give you a real number I'll stand behind."

## Write it down

Give them the offer in writing, with how long it is good for. It protects both sides and it signals that you are not going to move the goalposts while they think about it.

## The long game

You will lose trades to the guy who throws out an inflated number and quietly buries it in the financing. Let him have it. That customer figures it out eventually, and when they do, they remember who gave them a straight answer.
