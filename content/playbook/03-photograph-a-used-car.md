Two identical Malibus, same price, same miles. One gets calls, one sits. The difference is almost always the photos, and the fix is free.

## Background first, car second

The most common mistake is shooting a good car in front of a bad background — a dumpster, a chain link fence, the neighbor's trailer, three other cars crowding the frame.

Pick one spot on the lot and make it your photo bay. You want:

- The building or a clean wall behind the car, not clutter
- Nothing growing out of the roofline
- Enough room to walk all the way around
- The same spot every time, so your listings look like a set

That consistency is most of what makes a site look professional. Sixty cars shot in the same place reads as a real dealership. Sixty cars shot wherever they were parked reads as a guy with a phone.

## Get lower than you think

Phone at eye level makes a car look small and tall, like a rental listing. Drop to roughly headlight height — somewhere around your waist. Squat down. The car immediately looks longer, wider, and more planted.

This single change does more than any filter.

## The angle that sells

The money shot is the front three-quarter: stand off the front corner so you see the front and one full side. Turn the front wheel so the face of the wheel points at you, not the tire tread. It is a small thing that every manufacturer ad does and almost no independent lot bothers with.

## Shoot this order, every car, every time

1. Front three-quarter, driver side — this is your lead photo
2. Rear three-quarter, passenger side
3. Straight side profile
4. Straight front
5. Straight rear
6. Dash and gauges through the open driver door, engine running so the screens are lit
7. Front seats
8. Back seats
9. Trunk or bed, empty and clean
10. Engine bay
11. Odometer close-up
12. Tire tread

Twelve shots, about six minutes once you have the routine. The odometer and tread shots answer the two questions that generate the most phone calls, which means fewer tire-kickers and more real buyers.

## Clean beats new

A clean 2012 outsells a dirty 2016 in photos. Wash it. Wipe the dash. Pull the floor mats and shake them out. Take the paper plate and the air freshener off the mirror. Throw away the previous owner's gas receipts in the door pocket.

Ten minutes of cleaning is worth more than any camera.

## Time of day

Overcast is the best light you will ever get — it is a giant softbox and it kills harsh reflections. Bright noon sun is the worst: hard shadows, blown-out paint, and you standing in the reflection.

If you must shoot in sun, put the sun behind you and watch your own shadow does not fall across the car.

## What to do about flaws

Photograph them. A clear shot of the door ding means the person who drives over already knows about it. The alternative is finding out at delivery, which is how a sale turns into a refund request and a review.

## The one rule

Never use a manufacturer press photo or a stock image of a different car. The moment a customer realizes the photo is not the actual vehicle, everything else on your site becomes suspect. An honest "photos coming soon" costs you far less than a photo that turns out to be a lie.
