---
title: Phone photos on a wet Ohio afternoon — settings and order of shots
summary: Gray sky and a wet lot are not a problem. They are the best free lighting you get around here, if you know what to fix.
---

Middletown gives us a lot of flat gray days and a lot of wet pavement. Most people see that and wait for sun. Do not wait. Overcast is the light professionals pay to imitate, and wet asphalt turns into a mirror that makes a car look twice as expensive.

## Why gray is good

Direct sun creates hard reflections that fight the body lines and blow out light-colored paint. Clouds spread that light out. Colors stay true, chrome stops flaring, and the shape of the car actually reads.

The only thing overcast costs you is contrast, and that is easy to add back.

## Phone settings to fix first

**Turn off auto-HDR if your photos look flat and gray.** HDR lifts the shadows so much that black cars go muddy.

**Tap to focus on the car, then drag the exposure slider down slightly.** On a gray day your phone will try to make the sky properly bright, which makes the car dark. Pulling exposure down about a third protects the paint and deepens the sky. On a dark car in dim light, you may need to go the other way.

**Lock exposure (press and hold) before you walk the circle.** Otherwise every shot in the set has a different brightness and the listing looks stitched together.

**Use the 1x lens, not the ultra-wide.** Ultra-wide bends the car — the near end balloons, the far end shrinks. It makes a nice car look like a cartoon. Step back and use 1x.

**Wipe the lens.** It is a lot. Your phone lives in your pocket. One smear turns every shot hazy and you will not notice until you are at the desk.

## Use the water

Wet pavement is a free reflection. Shoot low and put the reflection in the frame — it fills the empty foreground and looks deliberate and expensive.

If it has stopped raining and the lot is drying in patches, park the car on a still-wet section. If it is fully dry and you want the effect, a bucket of water thrown in front of the car does the job.

## Rain that is still falling

Keep shooting, with two adjustments. Dry the car right before the set or the beading looks like neglect instead of polish. And keep water off the lens — a hat brim or the open trunk lid works as an umbrella.

Do not shoot the interior with wet hands and a wet door seal. Water spots on the dash photograph terribly.

## The order, in the weather

Work outside first, inside second — if the rain picks up you already have the shots that matter.

1. Front three-quarter, low, reflection in the frame
2. Rear three-quarter
3. Side profile
4. Wheels and tread
5. Any flaw, close and honest
6. Then open the doors and do the interior while the car is still dry inside

## Winter, specifically

Salt film is the enemy. A car that has been sitting through a February week has a white haze on the lower panels that reads as neglect in photos. Rinse the lower third of the car even if you do not have time for a full wash.

Also: shoot before four o'clock from November to February. After that the light is gone and everything you take will be yellow and grainy under the lot lights.

## What to do when the shots are bad

Reshoot. It takes six minutes. A bad photo set costs you weeks of the car sitting there, and every day it sits is money. There is no edit that saves a dark, crooked, ultra-wide photo of a dirty car in front of a dumpster.
