title: Financing talk for all-credit lots — legal and human
summary: What you can promise, what you absolutely cannot, and how to have the credit conversation without making someone feel small.
---

A good portion of our customers have been turned down somewhere before they walk in here. They arrive expecting to be embarrassed. How you handle the first two minutes of the credit conversation decides whether they trust anything else you say.

## What you cannot say

These are not style preferences. They are the phrases that draw regulatory attention and lawsuits:

- **"Guaranteed approval."** You do not approve anyone. A lender does.
- **"Everyone rides" / "no credit refused."** Same problem, dressed up.
- **Any specific rate before a lender has said it.** Not in an ad, not on the lot, not "you'll probably get around six."
- **A monthly payment stated as fact** before the terms exist.
- **"Your credit doesn't matter here."** It does, and saying otherwise sets up a bad surprise.

## What you can say, and it is plenty

- All credit types considered
- Approval and terms are set by the lender, not by us
- We work with lenders who do first-time buyers and rebuilt credit
- Here is what you can bring that improves your odds

That last one turns a passive conversation into something the customer can act on.

## How to ask about credit without the flinch

Do not ask for a score. Most people either do not know it or are ashamed of it, and the question sets the wrong tone.

Ask about the situation instead:

> "Anything in the last couple of years I should know about — repo, bankruptcy, anything still open? Not judging, it just changes which lender I send this to."

That is procedural, not personal. It gets honest answers because it is framed as routing, which is what it actually is.

## Set the down payment expectation early

The thing that sinks deals at the desk is a down payment surprise. Raise it before the test drive, not after:

> "Depending on how the approval comes back, they'll usually want something down. Do you have a range in mind?"

Now nobody has fallen in love with a car that was never going to work.

## Explain the tradeoff honestly

A longer term makes the payment smaller and the total bigger. Customers deserve to hear that in one sentence:

> "Seventy-two months gets you to the payment you want, but you'll pay more for the car overall and you'll be underwater longer. Sixty is the tighter payment but it saves you real money. Your call."

Most people pick the longer term anyway. That is fine — it is their decision, made with the facts. What is not fine is steering them there without mentioning it.

## First-time buyers

They often do not know what a lender looks at. Tell them plainly: steady income, time at the job, time at the address, and a down payment. Also tell them what does not matter as much as they fear — a thin file is not the same as a bad one.

## When the answer is no

Sometimes it is no. Do not string them along for a week hoping.

> "It didn't come back the way I wanted. Here's what would change it: a bigger down payment, or a co-signer, or about six months of on-time history. I'd rather tell you that than waste your time."

Give them the path. People come back to the person who told them the truth, and they tell other people about it.

## Write the disclosure the same way

Whatever is on the website has to match what you say on the floor. "All credit types considered, subject to lender approval" appears on our financing page and under the payment estimator for exactly that reason.
